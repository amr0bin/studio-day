Studies Teaching Writing Documentation

DocumentationOne of four

One camera position, every month

The work has to be recorded, and the recording has to cost almost nothing. Everything on this page exists so that documenting a month of studies takes one Monday and produces images that look like they belong to the same practice five years from now.

The reference

Florian Gadsby films his ceramics practice with a consistency that is the actual achievement. The camera sits in the same places, the light is the same light, the shots recur in the same order, and the finished pots are shown at the end in the same clear way each time. A viewer recognises a Gadsby video in two seconds without seeing a logo.

The transferable part is not the equipment. It is that every decision was made once. He is not choosing an angle each morning; he is putting the camera where the camera goes. That is what makes a documentation practice survivable alongside the making.

The setup, decided once

Position A
Overhead, flat to the tableThe working shot. Hands, paper, the piece as it develops. Fixed height, fixed crop.
Position B
Low and to the side, at table heightThe room shot. Materials, water, the edge of the paper. Used sparingly.
Position C
Square to the finished pieceThe record shot. Same distance, same crop, colour accurate. This is the image that sells the work and the one a jury sees.
Position D
The piece in the roomHanging, leaning, on the wall, with the wall left in frame. Not a record of the work but a record of the object existing somewhere. This is the one that belongs in a feed.
Position E
ScaleThe work with something in frame that gives its size: the table edge, a hand, the room. Always the second frame in a carousel, never the first.
Light
One source, same time of dayNorth light where possible. Consistency matters more than quality here, because a set of images taken under different light never reads as a set.
Audio
Recorded separatelyPeople forgive soft focus and not bad sound. The essay is the voiceover, so the audio carries the piece.
Naming
YYYY-MM-NNNYear, month, piece number. Matches the numbering on the back of the work.
Storage
Two places, one offlineRaw files and finished exports, backed up before the card is cleared.

What gets captured

The camera runs during the Sunday I would be working anyway. Filming is a switch I flip rather than a task I add, and what was worth keeping gets decided later.

  • Overhead footage across the studio day, running unattended.
  • Three or four still frames of work in progress, taken at the same points in the hour.
  • The record shot of each finished piece, taken at the close of the day.
  • A note in the studio log: hours, materials, what shifted.

One capture, three uses. The video goes to YouTube, the stills go into the series pages and the annual book, and the process footage becomes demonstration material inside a Foundations module.

The stage sequence

A process post from @nicolasuribeb runs four frames of a single figure: a sanguine gesture, a refined sanguine drawing, an ochre underpainting, then the resolved painting in cool greys. The crop is identical in every frame, so the four read as one movement rather than four pictures.

That is the still-frame format, already solved. Four fixed points in a piece, shot from Position A without moving anything, and the sequence assembles itself. It costs four shutter presses inside an hour I am working anyway, and it produces a post, a set of stills for the annual book, and demonstration material for a Foundations module from the same four frames.

The caption register is worth copying too. Plainly what it is and plainly what was hard: the mixed temperature lights were especially tough. No interpretation, no reaching. That is the one honest line each piece gets.

A third register is candid. Hélène Delmaire posts a work in progress and says she has been trying to paint animals in a way that is not tacky, and failing, for months. She names the passage she is happy with and the passage she cannot bring herself to touch. She corrects her own wording mid-caption when she thinks it was unfair. It is specific about failure without performing vulnerability, and it is the register my own writing is weakest in.

A carousel by @zbyneksedlecky makes the case for the scale frame. A commenter writes that it was a pleasant surprise to swipe and find out how big the painting really was. A photograph flattens size, and I will be posting 8 × 10 studies and large paintings from the same account, where a square crop makes them look identical. The first frame is the work. The second says how big it is.

Her posts are also crops rather than whole paintings. The frame lands where the paint is doing something. The whole piece is what the record shot is for; the feed can have the two inches that were difficult.

There is a quieter register available as well. A painting posted by @zbyneksedlecky carries one word, Untitled, and two hashtags, with the work photographed hanging on a rough plaster wall rather than shot flat. Both are honest. The process post explains, the in-situ post simply shows. Deciding which register a given month calls for is a two-second decision if the two positions already exist.

The page each month lands on

Ini Neumann runs a site with two things in the navigation, Archive and About. A project page is a run of photographs and then four lines of text: a fragment in italic, one plain sentence saying what the thing is, a credit, and a season and year. Nothing else.

That is a template rather than a style. The order never changes, so the writing takes five minutes and every page in the archive matches every other one. Dating by season and year instead of by day keeps the archive from reading as a feed.

One adjustment for my own work. Those captions are almost silent, and mine are long and descriptive because a photograph that only some readers can see is a photograph doing half its job. Both can hold: the visible caption stays to four lines, and the full description lives in the alt text where it belongs. Nobody has to choose between a spare page and a legible one.

The monthly rhythm

Documentation sits in the Monday rotation and takes two of the four Mondays in a month. The first Monday photographs and lists the studies. The second and third edit the video. The fourth is orders and correspondence.

One video a month, not one a week. Editing is the real cost in this whole system, and a rough monthly video that exists beats a polished quarterly one that does not.

Where it goes

  • YouTube. The monthly process video, narrated with that month's essay. Search-based, evergreen, no posting schedule.
  • The studies release. The record shot of each piece, in the same crop every month.
  • The annual book. Stills pulled from the year's footage in September.
  • The archive. Everything, named and backed up, whether or not it was used.

Still open

  • Camera and mount. Whatever gets used has to live set up, because a rig that needs assembling will not get used.
  • Whether the voiceover is read from the essay or spoken loosely from it.
  • Video length, and whether a fixed length helps or constrains.
  • Whether the record shot is photographed or scanned. Scanning is more accurate for work on paper and slower.

One thing worth watching. If the camera starts changing how I paint, that matters more than any of the above.
