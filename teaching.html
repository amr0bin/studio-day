FoundationsBegins 2028; designed now

Foundation year, reconsidered

Everything covered in Foundation year at Emily Carr, taken up again after fifteen years of learning design. One module a year, recorded only where a demonstration or a face earns the camera, eight hours to a module, and critique sessions in the second stage taught the way the shops were taught. Built so that teaching costs the practice as few Sundays as possible.

The pedagogy, in one statement

Understanding forms when something shifts in how a person thinks, works or sees a problem, and every design decision answers to that shift and to the evidence of what produced it. I learned this first in the shops at Emily Carr, where a student arrives with a problem in their own work and leaves with a way through it: knowledge forms through making, failing, iterating and receiving feedback, not through instruction alone. Neurobiology then showed me that the conditions that make learning stick are the conditions that support cognitive health: relevance, agency, social scaffolding and reflection, with emotional engagement as the mechanism by which experience becomes memory. A decade of learning design gave me the methods to act on both. So the designer's role is to architect conditions rather than deliver content: start from what the learner already knows, work backward from an observable outcome, build in formative feedback, treat accessibility as a design principle rather than an accommodation, and plan the evaluation before anything is built. Foundations is designed exactly this way. A lecture cannot produce the shift; a demonstration can show it once; a critique of the student's own attempt produces it every time. And because Foundation year is so often taught through comparison and a critique culture that treats the beginner as a problem to correct, the conditions here are built so that the material becomes learnable without that.

This paragraph is the teaching statement, drawn from the approach page. It goes in the readiness kit and answers the Emily Carr Continuing Studies postings as written.

The format, decided

Hours
Eight hours of content a module, not eighteenSix weeks at about eighty minutes. Eight hours is still twice what most artist-led courses offer, and it protects the Sundays.
What is filmed
Only what needs a demonstration or a faceA demonstration of the hand, the palette, the sitting: filmed. A talking-head where tone carries the point: filmed, short. Everything else is a page, a worksheet, an image sequence or a reference deck. Per week: about thirty minutes filmed, fifty on the page and at the easel.

About three hours of finished video a module, from roughly six hours of shootingFixed camera, one take where possible, a title card and nothing else. Recorded in July across Mondays and two halved Sundays; not across the spring.

Module one, the skeleton

Drawing from life, six weeks, eight hours. Each week has the same four parts so that the student always knows what a week is: a demonstration, a page, an attempt, and a question to bring to the crit.

Outcomes
By week six the student can: draw a figure from life in fifteen minutes with the proportions holding; resolve a one-hour drawing in five values and know when to stop; ask someone to sit and run the sittingThree, observable, each one visible in a drawing rather than described in a reflection.
Evaluation
The week-one gesture set against the week-six resolved drawing, plus one paragraph from the student on what changed in how they seeDecided before filming. The pair of drawings is the instrument; the paragraph is the evidence of the shift, and both come back to me with permission to use anonymised examples in the next module.
Access
Transcripts for every clip, described images, a text route through the whole module, and nothing timedThe pages-over-video decision is also an access decision: a page works on a phone, without sound, at any speed.

Why this stands

What it is for

Most online painting courses are built for painters who already know what they are missing: colour, composition, the head. Foundations is for people without a framework at all. Adults returning to art, painters who taught themselves, anyone who did a degree twenty years ago and wants to rebuild from the ground.

My claim on the material is specific. I went through that curriculum, then spent fifteen years studying how people actually learn, in health, higher education, research and the public sector. The second half is the part almost no artist teaching online can offer.

There is a further layer I have not decided about. Foundation year is often taught in ways that damage people: comparison, critique culture, the body treated as a problem to correct. My thesis is about exactly that. A version of Foundations could reconsider the conditions under which the material becomes learnable, and not only the material.

Why eight hours and not eighteen

Eighteen hours of finished video was the Foundation-year rhythm transposed directly, and it would have cost forty to sixty hours of editing, which is every Monday from March to July. The rhythm was never the point; the attempt and the adjustment were. Eight hours, of which three are filmed, keeps what the shop years proved and gives the spring back to the studies.
