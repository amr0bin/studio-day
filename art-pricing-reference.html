My pricing formula

My floor cost = materials per piece + (my hourly rate × hours per piece) + overhead per piece
My price = floor × 1.30 (minimum) to floor × 1.50 (maximum)
My price reviews = I periodically reassess my prices based on my costs, my practice, and what my work is doing — not on a fixed schedule, and never communicated as pressure to buyers
I never price anything below my floor. Each piece within an edition holds one consistent price for all copies.

How I review and adjust prices over time

↑
My price review approach
Applies to prints and originals · no increases between editions of the same work
Prints — edition of 10
Every print in an edition holds one consistent price for all 10 copies. When I release a new edition, I look back at the previous one. If at least 6 copies sold, I take that as a signal that my pricing is working and my practice is moving — and I set the new edition's price 10–15% higher. If fewer than 6 sold, I hold the price and think about whether the work, the format, or something else needs to shift first.

This is an internal review I do for myself. I don't communicate it to buyers.
Originals — per size and surface type
I track sales quietly by size and surface (e.g. all 8×10" linen pieces). When I feel my work at a given size and surface is consistently finding homes — roughly after 4–6 have sold — I raise my starting price for the next piece of that type by 10–15%. The increase reflects my growing practice, not a reaction to any individual sale.

I don't raise prices on work that's already listed. A piece holds its price until it sells or I retire it.

My channel adjustments

Direct (Shopify / my website): I use my prices as listed above.
Gallery or shop (40–50% commission): I gross up my price so my net is always protected.
My gallery list price = my target net ÷ (1 − commission rate)
Example: I want to net $300 → I list at $545 for a 45% commission gallery.
I keep my direct and gallery list prices publicly consistent. If a gallery lists a piece at $545, I match that price on my website — or I negotiate exclusivity terms with the gallery.

Why studies are priced lower than standard originals: Studies are process work — genuine and complete in themselves, but made as experimentation rather than as finished pieces. Pricing them at 40–50% of my equivalent standard original is honest and consistent. A study at $125–$150 is accessible to collectors who love my work but aren't ready for a $200+ original. It is not a discount — it is an accurate reflection of what the work is.

How I release them: I accumulate studies naturally as part of making each series. When I have enough to form a meaningful batch — whatever that looks like — I release them all at once as a drop on Shopify. No fixed schedule, no announcement in advance beyond listing them. I don't make print editions from studies — the study itself is the thing.

Floor cost note: Studies take less time than finished originals (~1–2 hrs vs 4–8 hrs) and use the same cotton paper. My floor is therefore lower — primarily materials (~$10–15 per sheet) plus ~1.5 hrs at $62/hr plus overhead share (~$83). Total floor: ~$62 per study at the low end of time.

How I release them: One pack per series or themed grouping. I list them permanently on Shopify — they are not limited editions or batch drops. When a pack sells out I decide whether to reprint based on demand. Shipping is passed to the buyer.

Tax — BC and Canada

◈
GST, PST, and what applies to my work
Verify specifics with a Canadian accountant familiar with artist taxation
GST — Federal (5%)
I only need to collect and remit GST once my worldwide revenue exceeds $30,000 CAD in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters. Below that threshold I am a "small supplier" and registration is optional. Once I cross it I must register with the CRA within 29 days and add 5% GST to Canadian sales.

The upside of registering: I can claim GST back on all my business expenses — materials, supplies, shipping, studio costs — which reduces what I owe. For US and international buyers, GST does not apply as these are exports.
BC PST (7%)
Original artwork created and sold by the artist is PST exempt in BC under the BC Provincial Sales Tax Act. This applies to my paintings and drawings. Limited edition fine art prints are generally also exempt as reproductions of my original work, but I will confirm this with the BC Ministry of Finance or a tax professional before collecting or exempting PST on prints.
Canadian tax deductions available to self-employed artists
As a self-employed artist working from a home studio I can deduct the following business expenses against my income — keeping receipts for all of these is essential:

Home studio (1/3 of apartment): I can deduct 1/3 of my rent, utilities, internet, and tenant's insurance as a business expense.

Art materials and supplies: Paints, paper, canvas, linen, panels, inks, brushes, printing costs — all deductible.

Finishing and shipping supplies: Glassine, backing boards, mailers, postage, packaging — all deductible.

Equipment and tools: Camera, lighting, computer used for my practice, editing software, website and Shopify fees — deductible (larger purchases may need to be depreciated over time).

Professional development: Art books, courses, workshops, museum memberships relevant to my practice — deductible.

Marketing and promotion: Kit email platform, Format website, photography of work, printing of promotional materials — deductible.

Business use of vehicle (if applicable): Trips to art supply stores, post office, galleries — deductible at the business-use percentage.

I file a T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities) with my personal tax return each year to report income and claim these deductions. Working with an accountant familiar with Canadian artist taxation is strongly recommended — there are nuances around the distinction between a business and a hobby under CRA rules, and maintaining consistent records of income and expenses protects me.
