# Statement

*Three lengths, revised each November alongside the question paragraph. Assembled 18 September 2026 from sentences already written across this repository, so the voice is hers rather than mine. Everything below is a draft to cut, not a text to adopt.*

**What the three are for.** The fifty words go in a prize entry, a caption, a bio line and an email signature. The two hundred go in a grant application, a residency form and a submission. The page goes in a portfolio, a proposal and a doctoral file. They say the same thing at three depths, and the short one is the hardest.

**The rule:** written for a jury, not for the thesis. Concrete before abstract. No sentence that could describe a different practice.

---

## Fifty words

> My subject is nature, and the body is part of what that word covers. We do not only live in nature; we are made of it, of water, mineral, weather and time. A face caught before it settles is wilderness too. I paint in pencil and acrylic, from life, on Sundays.

*Forty-nine words. The last sentence is the one to argue with: it trades an image for a fact, and a jury reading a hundred of these needs the fact. Test it both ways for a year.*

## One page

> My subject is nature, and the body is part of what that word covers. We do not only live in nature; we are made of it, of water, mineral, weather and time. A face caught before it settles is wilderness too.
>
> The question underneath the work is whether the ordinary body, in the ordinary day, is already enough, and whether painting can hold it without arguing for it. I am not interested in the body as a problem to be corrected, or in the face as a likeness to be achieved. I am interested in what is already there before either of those projects begins.
>
> **How it is made.** Pencil and acrylic, at two scales. Small studies at a fixed size, numbered from the first, made weekly and released monthly. Larger work on panel and linen, where a figure sits in a real room in daylight, built over several sessions. The ground is bright and left showing at the edges under a muted layer, so the surface keeps a record of how it was arrived at rather than concealing it.
>
> **Why from life.** I draw from sittings rather than from photographs. That decision costs time and produces fewer pictures, and it is the condition the work depends on: a sitting is an agreement between two people, held for three hours, in which one of them is looked at closely and the other has to keep looking. What the painting is about and how it is made are the same thing.
>
> **What runs beside it.** A written sequence on the body as the site of the sacred, written weekly from the studio and kept as a dated archive. The writing does not explain the paintings and the paintings do not illustrate the writing. They act on the same premise through different materials, which is why neither has to account for the other.
>
> **Where it comes from.** I trained at Emily Carr, entering at fifteen, and then spent years teaching inside its studios before leaving to study how people learn. That history is why access matters in this practice as a condition rather than as a theme: who gets to make art, under what conditions, and what a room does to a body that is being watched.

*Around three hundred and eighty words, which fits a page with room for an image. The last paragraph is the one to check hardest, because it is the only place biography is allowed, and it earns its place only if it explains the work.*

## To decide

- Whether "nature" or "the ordinary body" leads. Both are true; they attract different juries. My view is that nature leads for arts councils and prizes, and the ordinary body leads for anything academic or disability-adjacent. Two versions is acceptable; three is drift.
- Whether Dear Ordinary is named in the statement or kept separate. Currently separate.
- Whether the Emily Carr sentence survives. It is the only biographical claim here and it does real work, but a jury reading it as credentials rather than as cause is a failure.
