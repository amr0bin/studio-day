The studio day

Sunday, ten until six, at a table that takes up most of a small apartment. Monday evening, two to four hours after the day job, for writing and documenting and everything else. That is the week. Four projects run inside it, and nothing runs outside it.

The hours

Eight hours on Sunday. Two to four on Monday. Roughly thirty-five hours a month of making and between nine and seventeen of everything else, including shipping, correspondence, bookkeeping and editing.

Those are the real numbers, and every decision in these files was made against them. A project that needs a fifth thing does not get added. A project that turns Monday into a second job gets cut back until it fits. I would rather do four things at a depth I can stand behind than six at the surface.

The table is the constraint underneath the hours. It is where I work, and it is also where I eat, so the day has to close with the table clear. That has shaped the format of the work as much as any aesthetic decision.

Monday, after work

One block, two to four hours, holding everything that is not making. It is the bottleneck in the whole system, so it runs on a rotation rather than on whatever feels most urgent.

  • Week one. Photograph the finished pieces, number them, write a line for each, list them.
  • Week two. Video edit, first pass.
  • Week three. Video edit, finish and publish.
  • Week four. Orders, packing, post office, bookkeeping, correspondence.

The weekly essay is drafted and published on its own schedule, separate from this rotation. It is writing rather than admin, and it does not compete with shipping for the same hour.

Video editing is the item most likely to overrun. If a month gives me only two Mondays, the video is what waits.

The shape of the year

Studies run from January. Course preparation begins in spring and the module is recorded across the summer, when the studies drop to one Sunday a month. The writing runs the whole way through. In September the year's material starts being gathered, and the annual book is finished in November.

December is a retreat. It holds my birthday and the anniversary of a death in my family. Nothing launches, nothing ships, and nothing needs me. The November deadline exists so that stays true.

Where each part comes from

Each piece of this is modelled on someone already doing it well. Naming them keeps me from re-deriving decisions other people have already tested, and it keeps me honest about what I have proved and what I have only borrowed.

Writing beside the practice
Emma WebsterA process that runs sketch to maquette to virtual model to oil painting, tied to live questions about ecology and non-human seeing. In 2021 she published Lonescape, collected reflections on landscape and image-making. Her Decimate, at thirty-five dollars, is a sketchbook of the process behind a show rather than a catalogue of it.

Hélène DelmaireSmall-scale alla prima oil, with prints released on a named day and hour translated into three time zones. The captions say what is failing, what is pleasing, and what she cannot bring herself to touch yet.

@nicolasuribebA single figure shown across four frames, sanguine gesture through to resolved painting, at an identical crop. The caption says what it is and what was difficult, and nothing else.

Heather DayThe landing page is the exhibition record itself, newest first, with press listed as an event of the same order. Four nav items, a one-sentence bio, and a studio inquiry link with the subject and opening line already written for the sender.
The archive over time
Samantha HaringRecent years listed flat, 2021 through 2026, with older work collapsed into named series carrying date ranges. One project, Mapping the Studio, runs from 2015 to now and is subdivided by the studio she was in at the time.
The project page
Ini NeumannA whole site of two nav items, Archive and About. Each project page is a run of photographs followed by four lines: a fragment in italic, one plain sentence saying what it is, a credit, and a season and year.

Grants, and studies as archive
Flora YukhnovichTwo Elizabeth Greenshields Foundation Awards, in 2013 and 2016, then a residency and a school prize, before the solo shows. Zoey Frank has held Greenshields grants too. Her studies also sit in the navigation as their own section, level with the paintings.

The Greenshields Foundation is a Montreal charity founded in 1955, funding representational painting, drawing, printmaking and sculpture at the study or early-career stage. In 2025 it awarded close to C$1.6 million across ninety-three artists and students. It does not fund abstract or non-objective work, which makes figurative practice an advantage rather than an exception.

One caution before I spend time on an application. A grant aggregator lists an age limit of eighteen to forty-one at first application, and states the grants are not intended for well-established artists. I have not found that limit stated by the Foundation itself, so it needs checking directly against their criteria first.
