Why these numbers differ from my other documents

Two corrections sit underneath this page. Both matter, and they pull in opposite directions, which is why the result is close to — but not the same as — a simple read of my example scenario.

My example scenario reserves 20% income tax + 11.9% CPP = 31.9% of every dollar of profit. Whether that is accurate depends on something the scenario doesn't state: do I have other income?

If my practice is my only income and it earns around $13,000 a year, the federal basic personal amount ($16,452 for 2026) and the BC basic personal amount ($13,216) both exceed my income. I pay no income tax at all — only CPP, at 11.9% on earnings above the $3,500 basic exemption. That works out to roughly 8–9%, not 31.9%.

If I have a day job at around $40,000, my personal amounts are already used up by that salary. Every dollar of art income is then taxed at the margin: 14% federal + 5.6% BC + 11.9% CPP, less the small deduction and credit for half my CPP. That lands at roughly 31% — almost exactly my 31.9% assumption.

So the reserve in my other documents isn't wrong. It is the right number for an artist with a day job, and a very conservative one for an artist whose practice is their whole income. The Other income field above switches between the two.

Reserving too much is a mild problem, not a serious one — the money is still mine. But it makes the target look further away than it is.

My floor formula is materials + (hourly rate × hours) + overhead. The $62/hr labour component is the right way to price — it makes sure my time is paid for. But it is not money that leaves the business. It is my pay.

That means the "profit" figure in my example scenario is not my take-home, and it is not my taxable income either. As a sole proprietor I can't deduct a salary to myself. My taxable income on the T2125 is revenue minus real cash costs — materials, studio rent share, printing, packaging, subscriptions, Shopify fees. The labour charge never appears.

This page is built on cash, which is why the breakdown above shows fixed costs as a single quarterly block rather than folded into a per-piece floor. It is the same money, counted once, in the form the CRA and my bank account both recognise.

A useful coincidence: at low sell-through the labour I'd have added back is small, while my fixed costs are large and constant. The two roughly cancel, which means my example scenario's "profit" figure is a reasonable proxy for taxable income at this scale. It stops being a good proxy as sell-through climbs.

CPP — self-employed. 11.9% on net business income above the $3,500 basic exemption, up to the YMPE of $74,600. Half is deductible from income; the other half generates a non-refundable tax credit. Both are modelled here.

Federal income tax. The lowest bracket is 14% for 2026 (reduced from 15%, prorated at 14.5% for 2025). The basic personal amount is $16,452.

BC income tax. Budget 2026 raised the lowest rate from 5.06% to 5.60% on the first $50,363 — the first increase to BC's bottom rate in decades. The basic personal amount is $13,216, and the credit rate rose to 5.60% to match.

BC tax reduction credit. Budget 2026 raised this to $690. It is fully available below $25,570 of net income, phases out at 3.56% above that, and reaches zero at $44,952. At my income level this wipes out any BC tax that survives the basic personal amount — worth knowing, and easy to miss.

Worth flagging for 2027 onwards: BC has paused indexation of its brackets and credits from 2027 to 2030, freezing them at 2026 levels. As my income grows over those four years, more of it drifts into higher brackets with no offsetting threshold growth. This page will need revisiting each year.

Everything omitted here works in my favour. I've left it out so the target is a floor rather than a hope.

  • Capital Cost Allowance on my startup gear. The MacBook, camera, lights, and tripod — roughly $4,500–5,000 — are depreciated over several years and reduce my taxable income meaningfully in the early years.
  • The Canada Workers Benefit. A refundable credit for low-income workers, including the self-employed. At the income levels on this page I would likely qualify for a real amount. My accountant can confirm.
  • GST Input Tax Credits. Once registered, I claim back the GST I pay on materials, subscriptions, printing, and packaging.
  • Higher-priced surfaces. This page prices every original as cotton paper, my base. Linen adds $20–30 and cradled panel adds $50–70 per piece, at almost no extra cash cost.
  • Price reviews. A print edition that sells 6 of 10 copies launches its successor 10–15% higher. Compounding across series, this lowers the sell-through I need over time.

What is not included on the other side: shipping, which I pass to the buyer, and any gallery commission, which would need grossing up per my pricing reference.

A note for whoever is helping me with this. This page is a planning tool, not a filing document. It models tax rather than reserving a flat percentage, which makes it more accurate but also more sensitive to assumptions — particularly whether I have other income. Before I rely on a number here for anything consequential, it goes to my accountant. And nothing on this page changes my pricing: I never quote below the floor in my pricing reference, and I never change the price on a piece that's already listed.
