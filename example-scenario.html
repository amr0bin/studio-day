My responsibilities — what running this practice actually requires

☰
What I commit to doing to make this work
Organised by area · realistic and honest about the time involved
  • ✦
    Make the work 6 studio days per month, consistently. I produce 6 pieces per month and choose 1 as the postcard image for a card pack design. The work itself is the foundation of everything else. No marketing substitutes for a strong, consistent body of work.
  • ◷
    Track my time and costs accurately I keep a simple log of actual hours per piece and actual materials costs each month. This is how I refine my floor costs and make sure my pricing stays honest. Without this, my numbers are guesses.
  • ◻
    Photograph and list work promptly Good photography of my work is non-negotiable. I photograph each piece when it's finished, write a short honest description, and list it on Shopify within a few days of completion. Sitting on finished work delays income unnecessarily.
  • ✉
    Pack and ship card pack orders promptly When a card pack order comes in, I ship within 3–5 business days. Each pack is wrapped neatly, backed with a board, placed in a rigid mailer, and includes a thank you card. This is how people become repeat buyers — a good unboxing experience matters even at an accessible price point.
  • ↗
    Pack and ship orders carefully and promptly Every print ships in my finishing kit: glassine envelope, acid-free backing board, rigid mailer, and a handwritten thank you card. I aim to ship within 3–5 business days of an order. This is how people become repeat buyers — not from marketing, but from a genuinely good experience receiving my work.
  • ◈
    Send quarterly emails to my mailing list Four times a year, I send a simple honest email through Kit: what I've been making, what's available, what's coming. No sales language. No countdown timers. Just a note from me about my work. This is how I stay in touch with people who care about what I do.
  • ▶
    Post YouTube process videos when I have something genuine to share I don't post on a schedule for its own sake. When I have a process, a idea, or a story worth sharing, I film and edit it. YouTube's search-based discovery means a good video keeps finding people long after I post it. Quality over frequency.
  • ☰
    Keep clean financial records every month I record all income and expenses monthly, keep all receipts (digital or photographed), download my Shopify statements, and file everything in a folder organised by year and month. At tax time I hand this to my accountant — it should require no heroic effort because I've stayed on top of it throughout the year.
  • ◈
    File my taxes correctly and on time I file a T2125 with my personal T1 return each year, claiming all legitimate deductions. I pay any balance owing by April 30. I monitor my revenue against the $30,000 GST threshold and register proactively when approaching it. I work with a Canadian accountant familiar with artist taxation.
  • ↑
    Review my prices periodically After each print edition, I check how many sold. If at least 6 of 10 copies sold, my next edition for that size launches at 10–15% more. For originals, I review prices per surface type as my practice develops. This is a private, internal review — I never communicate it to buyers as a sales tactic.

How I optimise financially — without compromising my integrity

My approach to financial optimisation is simple: make the most of what I'm already doing, price my work honestly, reduce unnecessary costs, and keep more of what I earn through legitimate tax deductions. I don't use pressure tactics, artificial scarcity, countdown timers, or manipulative language of any kind — ever.
◈
Pricing and income optimisation
Things I do to earn fairly from my work — all grounded in real value
  • 1
    Price prints first, originals second Prints generate ~$11,400 of this scenario's $12,849 profit because the floor cost is low and the volume is high. Prints are my most efficient income source. I make sure every original has a print edition — this is the single biggest financial lever I have, and it requires no compromise at all.
  • 2
    Raise prices at the right moments, not randomly When a print edition sells well (6+ of 10), my next edition launches 10–15% higher. When originals of a given size and surface sell consistently, I raise my starting price for the next piece of that type. These increases reflect my growing practice and track record — they are honest and earned.
  • 3
    Treat card packs as my most accessible entry point Card packs are my lowest price point and the easiest way for someone new to my work to own a piece of it. A buyer who starts with a $42 card pack and loves it is a future print or original buyer. I keep them well-stocked and easy to find on my Shopify store.
  • 4
    Build my mailing list consistently Email converts better than any social platform. Every person who buys from me, signs up from my website, or finds me through YouTube is a potential list subscriber. I grow this list steadily and treat it with care — four honest emails a year about real work, nothing more.
  • 5
    Pass shipping costs to the buyer I charge actual shipping at checkout rather than absorbing it into my prices or offering free shipping. This protects my margins, especially on international orders where Canada Post rates are significant ($45+). Buyers understand this — it is standard practice for independent artists.
  • 6
    Offer card pack buyers a loyalty path Card pack buyers who move on to prints or originals are my most engaged collectors. I don't offer a formal discount programme — I simply make it easy for them to find my other work from the same shop, and let the quality of the work do the rest.
  • 8
    Release studies as irregular batch drops I make studies as part of my practice anyway — they are already being created. When a natural batch accumulates, I photograph them, list them all at once on Shopify, and let them find buyers at their own pace. Studies are priced at $125 minimum — honest and genuinely profitable since their floor cost is ~$62 ($125–$150 for a 4×6" on cotton paper · minimum selling price $125) — honest, accessible, and genuinely profitable since their floor cost is lower. No schedule, no announcement pressure, no print editions. The study is the thing.
  • 9
    Sell card packs — printed reproductions, always profitable A recurring membership where I lose $6–11 per member per month is not sustainable. Instead, I sell printed art card packs via Shopify — quality 4×6" reproductions of my work on matte card stock, sold in packs of 6 at $42–$50 or packs of 8 at $52–$62. My cost per pack of 6 is ~$10–12 including printing, packaging, and Shopify fees, giving me a margin of ~$30–38 per pack. Buyers get something beautiful and affordable. I earn rather than lose on every transaction. No ongoing fulfilment commitment.
  • 10
    Gross up prices for galleries and shops If a gallery or shop takes 40–50% commission, I calculate my list price so that my net after commission equals what I would earn selling direct. I never give a gallery my direct price — that would mean earning half what my work is worth. My gallery list price = my target net ÷ (1 − commission rate).

Tax and expense optimisation
Keeping more of what I earn through legitimate deductions — see my full tax reference document
Claim my home studio
I deduct 1/3 of my rent, utilities, and internet as a business expense — this is the single largest annual deduction available to me. At $1,500+ rent, that's $500+/month or $6,000+/year off my taxable income.
Deduct all materials
Every paint, canvas, panel, paper, ink, and brush is a deductible business expense. I keep every receipt. At $375/month in materials, that's $4,500/year in deductions.
Deduct platform fees
Shopify fees ($925 in this scenario alone), Format subscription, Kit email platform, and domain costs are all deductible. I save all invoices automatically.
Buy supplies before December 31
If I need materials, equipment, or supplies for my practice, buying before year-end means I deduct them in the current tax year rather than waiting until next year.
Register for GST at the right time
Once registered, I claim back GST on all business expenses (Input Tax Credits). On $375/month in materials alone, that's roughly $225/year back from the CRA — plus GST on all other deductible expenses.
Deduct my accountant
The cost of my accountant is itself a deductible business expense. Good tax advice pays for itself — the deductions it protects far outweigh the fee.

Realistic expectations — what this scenario assumes and what might differ

◈
What to expect vs. this best-case model
Honest context for a first launch
  • ~
    Full sell-out in 3 months is optimistic for a first launch A more realistic first-series sell-through rate is 50–70%. At 60% sell-through, profit would be approximately $7,700 — still a strong result. The scenario above is a ceiling, not a guarantee. Each subsequent series tends to sell faster as my audience grows.
  • ~
    Originals may take longer to sell than prints Prints are more accessible in price and collectors buy them more readily. Originals require more trust and often more time. I don't worry if an original sits for a few months — I don't discount it or chase the sale. I let it find the right person at the right time.
  • ~
    My audience needs time to build 193 visitors/month at launch with no active shop is a seed, not a crowd. The mailing list and YouTube channel both take time to develop. Series 2 and 3 will reach more people than Series 1 — this is normal and expected. Consistency over time is the strategy.
  • ~
    Floor costs may be higher or lower than estimated My floor costs use midpoint estimates. Once I track actual hours and actual materials spend for one full series, I'll have real numbers to work with. If my floors are higher, I adjust prices accordingly — I never sell below floor.
