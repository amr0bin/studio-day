WritingDear Ordinary

The asking is the thing

Fifty-six weeks of a thesis inquiry that moves between scholarly writing and the studio, published weekly. This is what it is about and why I keep coming back to it.

A kitchen in Antigonish

My grandmother put a brush in my hand when I was three. I do not remember being taught anything. I remember being allowed.

Everything I make now is evidence that the girl at that table came through. The hands are the same hands. Whatever happened in the years between, they kept working, and the work is the proof rather than the memory of it.

That is not the reason I paint. It is the reason I cannot stop.

What the inquiry asks

Is the body you are living in, the ordinary one, in the ordinary day, already enough?

Western contemplative traditions have largely treated the body as the problem. The site of distraction and disorder, to be subdued or transcended on the way to something higher. That posture has consequences for actual bodies, and it does not fall evenly. The bodies most urgently required to transcend their materiality are the ones already marked as excessive, insufficient, or wrong. Meanwhile the bodies taken as the spiritual norm are rarely named as bodies at all. They simply pass as the human.

So the question is never the body. It is which body, whose body, and who decides when a body is spiritual enough to count.

The inquiry runs across eight constellations of equal dignity, thirty-five stars, no hierarchy and no fixed sequence. It spirals rather than progresses, which is the argument as much as the structure. A path with a destination sorts people by how far along it they are. A recursive process of recognition does not.

Facts are where the truth is kept

Each week opens with an artist in the middle of something specific. A material choice, a gesture, a decision recorded in the work. Lee Krasner taking scissors to her own paintings in 1955, cutting up drawings she had laboured over for years and reassembling them with the joins visible. Cecilia Vicuña knotting quipus from debris pulled out of gutters, taking up a form the Third Council of Lima ordered destroyed in 1583.

The framework comes after. Interoception, polyvagal theory, attachment research, the via negativa. It arrives to name something the artists have already shown, and the reader has already felt by the time it is named.

The reach past the fact only works if the fact is exact. A sentence that gestures at profundity while getting the date wrong has forfeited the thing it was reaching for. So every biographical detail, every quotation, every critical position goes to the record before it goes to the page, and where a claim rests on lived experience that is not mine, the interpreting is done by the artist's own verified words or by scholarship from inside that experience. I write as a white settler in Canada of Celtic descent. That is a location to write honestly from, and it draws a boundary around what my voice has standing to say.

Living inside a question long enough to build it a place to stand

Three people show me how this is done.

Maria Popova weaves thinkers across centuries until they are in conversation, and the essays close on a question larger than the one they opened with. She is on a search for meaning and she has not resolved it. The writing stays alive on that. I am doing the same search from a different room.

Emma Webster builds whole worlds before she paints them. Sketches become handmade maquettes, the maquettes are scanned and rebuilt as virtual models, the models are lit like a stage set, and only then does oil go onto canvas. She has said that painting has always collaged its sources and has rarely been purely observational. What that process looks like from outside is patience. What it looks like from inside, I think, is refusing to leave a question until it has somewhere to stand.

Heather Day works intuitively and slowly, following what the elements around her are doing. She lives and works in the Mojave Desert now, in the light and the shadow she paints from, immersed in the thing itself. That is the part that undoes me. She arranged her life so the work could happen inside its own conditions.

Mine are the Pacific Northwest, the ocean and the trees, and the light here that is soft for eight months and then suddenly is not. My subject is nature and we are made of it, water and mineral and weather and time. The difference is that the nature I am looking at is human. A face caught before it settles is wilderness too.

The turning back toward

What builds a child's sense of security is not the absence of rupture. It is the experience of repair, lived over and over, until the body learns that when this breaks it can be mended. Mary Main's work on adult attachment found that what predicts a child's security is not whether a parent's own history was free of rupture, but whether they can tell that history coherently, having made some sense of it. The literature calls this earned security. A person handed a childhood of breaks that were never repaired is not sentenced by it.

Some institutions never turn back. The church that condemned does not return. The family that rejected does not. A system that demonstrates repeatedly that rupture is permanent teaches its own lesson, and a body learns that lesson well.

Coming back to this work, week after week, is the most intimate relationship in my life. Part of why is that it is not only mine. The more courage I find to look directly at the personal, the more of the human condition I am standing inside rather than beside. The specific is the only route there. Abstraction keeps everyone at a polite distance, which is what it is for.

This writing will accompany the practice for as long as I am able to do it. I hope that is the rest of my life.

Not the answer

I wrote a poem this year for Rebecca Elson, an astronomer and poet who died at thirty-nine. The claim in it, in its simplest form, is that you are already made of the thing you thought you were seeking. The iron in your blood was forged in a dying star and crossed three billion years to arrive here, carrying oxygen to these particular thoughts in this particular dark.

The asking is the thing. Not the answer. The asking.

Fifty-six weeks will not settle whether the ordinary body is enough. What they can do is ask it more precisely, in front of specific work, with the studio as the place the question gets tested in materials rather than argued in sentences. And then the question gets asked again, of a different body, on a different Sunday, by hands that have been doing this since a kitchen in Antigonish.

The form each week takes

Two or three artists a week, usually mixing registers rather than repeating one. A historical figure alongside contemporary ones, or a canonical name alongside someone working outside institutional recognition. They have to share a gesture rather than a topic, or the weave is decorative.

The structure runs: an artist in the middle of something, the week's question named obliquely, the framework held lightly, a second artist reaching back to touch the first, a gathering, and a studio invitation written peer to peer. Keywords and anchors close it.

The register is the part that takes discipline. Ground prose carries the facts, the sequence, the material and the dates, and it is most of any post. The sentences that reach are scarce, roughly one in eight. A lyrical sentence arriving after six plain ones is a change in altitude a reader feels. The same sentence after six others like it is noise.

The diagnostic pass

Habits of voice are invisible from inside a draft and obvious from a count. All six before a piece is finished.

0 of 6 complete

A first draft is expected to fail this. That is a property of fluent prose rather than a sign the draft is bad, and the pass is mandatory rather than contingent.

Every biographical detail, quotation, date and critical position is checked against a real source before publishing, however well-sourced a draft appears.

Where the writing lives

Patreon holds the published essays. It sits behind a login, which is a real improvement over an open platform, though nothing makes writing uncopyable. What Patreon does not do is protect against loss, so a copy of every essay is kept outside it.

Substack was the previous home. I left because the work was open to crawling. YouTube is not an alternative for the same reason, at a larger scale, and it is not a writing surface in any case.

The annual book

Each year the writing, the process documentation and the works are gathered into one volume, and the volume is sold. Closing the chapter is what it is for. Selling it is how it reaches anyone.

Her Decimate is the nearer model for the object itself. Thirty-five dollars, described as a digital sketchbook, published on the occasion of her 2026 show at Petzel, showing the evolution of the sculptures and scenes behind the paintings. It carries a title and an epigraph rather than a year. A sketchbook rather than a catalogue: the material that did not become the finished work, which is cheaper to produce, better to read, and does not compete with selling the paintings.

Emma Webster's Lonescape, published in 2021, is the closest precedent I have found. Collected reflections on landscape and image-making in a heavily digital world, standing beside the paintings as its own object rather than documenting them. Florian Gadsby's annual book records a year of making; hers holds a year of thinking. Dear Ordinary is closer to the second, and the volume should be built as reflection with the work inside it rather than as a catalogue with essays appended.

Nothing in it is made for it. The essays are already written, the process stills come from the monthly videos, and the works are photographed as they are finished. Gathering begins in September, layout runs through October, and the book is finished in November so that December stays clear.

Since it is for sale, some of this has to be settled rather than left open. Print on demand keeps inventory out of a small apartment and puts a copy in someone's hands without me holding stock or shipping it, at the cost of a higher unit price and less control over paper. Short-run digital printing gives a better object and means boxes under the table. Thirty-five US dollars is Webster's price with a gallery behind it; something in the region of forty Canadian is plausible for a softcover of a hundred pages or so, and the printing quote decides whether that holds.

Trim size, page count, whether the writing appears whole or selected, and whether it carries an ISBN and a deposit with Library and Archives Canada are still open. Fixing the format once means the volumes read as a set on a shelf and I stop deciding it annually.
