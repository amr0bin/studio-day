Tax & Deductions Reference

Self-employed artist · British Columbia, Canada · All figures CAD

Important: This is a reference guide based on current CRA and BC tax rules, not professional tax advice. I will verify specifics with a Canadian accountant familiar with artist taxation before filing — particularly around PST on prints, and the business vs. hobby distinction.

Step 1 — Establish myself as a business, not a hobby

⚑
This is the most important distinction I need to make
The CRA distinguishes between a business and a hobby — only a business can deduct expenses

The CRA looks for a reasonable expectation of profit to classify my art practice as a business. I don't need to be profitable yet — I just need to demonstrate I'm operating with the intention of making money. Here's how I do that:

  • 1
    Keep a separate bank account for my art income and expenses. This is the single clearest signal to the CRA that I'm running a business. I open one now, before I start earning.
  • 2
    Track all income and all expenses from day one. Every sale, every material purchase, every subscription. A simple spreadsheet is fine to start.
  • 3
    Keep all receipts. Physical or digital — I photograph paper receipts and save them in a folder organised by month. No receipt means no deduction.
  • 4
    File a T2125 each year. This is the CRA form (Statement of Business or Professional Activities) where I report my art income and claim my deductions. It goes with my personal T1 tax return.
  • 5
    Consider registering a business name. Not legally required as a sole proprietor, but it adds credibility. I can register a business name in BC for a small fee through BC Registry Services.

Step 2 — Understand when I need to collect GST

◈
GST — Federal 5%
I don't collect this until I cross the $30,000 threshold
Right now (under $30,000)
I am a "small supplier" — I do not collect or remit GST. My prices are my prices. No action needed on GST until I cross the threshold.
Once I cross $30,000
I must register for a GST number within 29 days of crossing the threshold. I then add 5% GST to Canadian sales and remit it to the CRA quarterly or annually.
The upside of GST registration: Once registered, I can claim back the GST I pay on all my business expenses — materials, supplies, shipping, equipment. This is called an Input Tax Credit (ITC) and it can meaningfully reduce what I owe.

US and international buyers: GST does not apply to exports. I never add GST to sales shipped outside Canada, regardless of my registration status.

My threshold tracker: I watch my cumulative Canadian revenue across any 12-month rolling period. The moment I approach $30,000 I register proactively — registering late carries penalties.

Step 3 — Understand BC PST on my work

◈
BC PST — 7%
Original artwork I create and sell is PST exempt — prints need confirmation
  • ✓
    Original paintings and drawings: PST exempt in BC under the BC Provincial Sales Tax Act. As the artist creating and selling my own original work, I do not collect PST on these. No action needed.
  • ?
    Limited edition fine art prints: Generally also exempt as reproductions of my original work, but this is a grey area I will confirm directly with the BC Ministry of Finance (1-877-388-4440) or with my accountant before selling prints.
  • →
    Card packs (printed reproductions): Printed art card packs sold as goods are likely subject to PST as a tangible personal property sale — I confirm this with the BC Ministry of Finance or my accountant before selling.
BC Ministry of Finance — PST enquiries: 1-877-388-4440 (toll-free) · Monday to Friday, 8:30am – 4:30pm Pacific
Online: gov.bc.ca/pst

Step 4 — Claim every deduction I'm entitled to

↓
My deductible business expenses
All claimed on my T2125 · keep receipts for everything · percentages apply where mixed personal/business use
Category What I can deduct How it works
Home studio
1/3 of my apartment
 1/3 of my monthly rent, utilities (heat, electricity, water), internet, and tenant's insurance I calculate the studio as a percentage of my total apartment square footage and apply that percentage to each bill. I keep a floor plan or measurement record to support the claim.
Art materials
Everything I make work with
 Paints, inks, paper, canvas, linen, panels, gesso, brushes, palette knives, mediums, varnish, and any other materials used to create my work 100% deductible. I keep all receipts from art supply stores. If I buy in bulk, I deduct in the year of purchase.
Printing costs
Limited edition prints
 Paper and printing service fees for my limited edition prints 100% deductible as a cost of producing inventory for sale.
Packaging and shipping
Everything that leaves with the work
 Glassine envelopes, backing boards, rigid mailers, boxes, corner protectors, foam, thank you cards, postage, and Canada Post fees 100% deductible. I keep receipts from Uline, Canada Post, and any other suppliers.
Website and platforms
My online presence
 Format subscription, Shopify subscription and transaction fees, domain registration, Kit email platform 100% deductible as business operating costs. I save invoices from each platform monthly.
Equipment
Tools of my practice
 Camera and lenses (for photographing work), lighting equipment, computer, tablet, editing software, storage drives If used exclusively for business: 100% deductible. If mixed personal/business use (e.g. my computer): I deduct the business-use percentage only. Larger purchases over ~$500 may need to be depreciated (Capital Cost Allowance) rather than fully expensed in year one — my accountant will guide this.
Professional development
Growing my practice
 Art books, technique books, online courses, workshops, classes, museum and gallery memberships where relevant to my practice 100% deductible when directly relevant to my art practice. I keep a note of why each purchase relates to my work.
Marketing
Promoting my work
 Photography of my work (if hired out), printing of promotional materials, business cards, any paid promotion 100% deductible. My YouTube channel costs (any equipment purchased specifically for it) are also deductible.
Bank and payment fees
Cost of getting paid
 Business bank account fees, Shopify payment processing fees (2.9% + 30¢), any PayPal or Stripe fees 100% deductible. Shopify fees are automatically recorded in my Shopify dashboard — I download these annually.
Professional fees
Getting expert help
 Accountant fees for preparing my T2125 and tax return, any legal fees related to my business 100% deductible. This means the cost of hiring an accountant is itself a deductible expense.
Vehicle
If applicable
 Trips to art supply stores, the post office, galleries, framing shops, and other business-related destinations I keep a mileage log (date, destination, purpose, km). I deduct the business-use percentage of my total vehicle costs, or use the CRA per-kilometre rate. Personal trips are not deductible.

Step 5 — Keep records the CRA expects

☰
What I keep and for how long
CRA requires business records to be kept for 6 years from the end of the tax year they relate to
  • All sales records — Shopify order history, Format sales, any direct sales. I download and save these annually.Keep for 6 years
  • All receipts for expenses — physical or photographed. Organised by month in a folder (digital or physical).Keep for 6 years
  • Bank and credit card statements — for my dedicated art business account.Keep for 6 years
  • Lease or rental agreement — to support my home studio deduction claim.Keep current copy plus 6 years
  • Mileage log — if I deduct vehicle use. Date, destination, purpose, kilometres.Keep for 6 years
  • Filed tax returns (T1 + T2125) — copies of what I submitted each year.Keep indefinitely
  • GST returns — once registered, copies of all returns filed.Keep for 6 years
Simple system I use: One folder per year, with subfolders for each month. Every receipt goes in immediately — photographed with my phone and saved to the folder the same day I spend the money. At year end I export my Shopify and bank statements and hand everything to my accountant.

Key dates and actions

◷
My annual tax calendar
Self-employed in Canada · personal tax year runs January 1 – December 31
  • Jan
    Organise last year's records. Compile all receipts, download Shopify and bank statements for the full prior year. Hand to accountant or begin T2125 preparation.
  • Mar
    File my T1 personal return by April 30 (or June 15 if self-employed — but any tax owed is still due April 30). Filing late when I owe money triggers a 5% penalty plus 1% per month interest.
  • Apr
    Pay any balance owing by April 30. Even if I file in June as a self-employed person, I pay what I owe by April 30 to avoid interest.
  • Jun
    Self-employed filing deadline: June 15. I can file my return by June 15 without a late-filing penalty, but only if I owe nothing or have already paid by April 30.
  • Sep
    Review GST revenue threshold. Check my rolling 12-month Canadian revenue. If approaching $30,000, I register for GST proactively rather than scrambling at the deadline.
  • Dec
    Year-end expense review. If I need to purchase materials, supplies, or equipment for my practice, buying before December 31 means I can deduct it in the current tax year.

Quarterly tax instalments

◈
I may need to pay tax in instalments once my income grows
The CRA requires instalments if I owe more than $3,000 in net tax in the current year and either of the two previous years

If the CRA determines I owe instalments, they will send me an instalment reminder in February and August. Instalment due dates are March 15, June 15, September 15, and December 15 each year. Missing instalments doesn't mean I can't pay at year end — it just means I may owe instalment interest on the shortfall. My accountant will help me determine the right instalment amount once my income is established.

Find a Canadian accountant familiar with artist taxation

What to look for: A CPA (Chartered Professional Accountant) with experience in self-employed creative professionals or artists. Ask specifically whether they've worked with artists claiming home studio deductions and T2125 filings.

Where to find one: CPA Canada's Find a CPA directory at cpаcanada.ca · local arts councils sometimes maintain referral lists · word of mouth from other self-employed artists in Vancouver is often the best route.

What to bring to the first meeting: A summary of my expected income sources (prints, originals, studies, card packs), my apartment lease showing rent amount, and a rough list of monthly expenses. Most accountants offer a free initial consultation.
