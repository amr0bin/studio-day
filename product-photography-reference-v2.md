# Product photography — the look, and how to rebuild it

*Andrea Robin Studio · September 2026 · v2*

A record of how the reference photography is actually made, and how much of it transfers to
flat work. Written so the setup can be reconstructed rather than rediscovered.

---

## Status of this document

**The reference** is We Are Studio Studio (`wearestudiostudio.com/collections/ceramics`),
founded by Ini Neumann in Hamburg. Wheel-thrown stoneware, sold direct. Several product
image filenames carry the prefix `annahaerlin`, so the photography appears to be credited
to Anna Haerlin.

**v1 was written blind.** The site's images could not be fetched, so the specification was
derived from optics rather than observation. **v2 is written from nine reference images**,
and three of v1's guesses were wrong. Those corrections are listed below rather than quietly
overwritten, because knowing which reasoning failed is more useful than a clean document.

## Corrections to v1

**1. The gradient is made with one light, not two.**
v1 said the backdrop had to be lit separately from the subject by a second light or bounce.
It doesn't. There is a single soft source, and the gradient is ordinary fall-off across the
sweep: brightest near the light, darkening toward the far corners, with a further soft
darkening where the sweep curves from horizontal to vertical. That curve is visible as a
faint horizon in the upper third of several shots. **[Observed]**

This is cheaper and simpler than the v1 spec. One light, one sweep.

**2. The crop is portrait, not square.**
v1 guessed square for a Shopify grid. Measured across all nine images, the ratio sits between
0.891 and 0.906, clustering at about **0.90, or 9:10**. Only slightly taller than square, and
noticeably squarer than the usual 4:5. **[Verified]** by measurement, with the caveat that
these are screenshots of a rendered container rather than original files, so the true ratio
may differ slightly.

**3. The ground is warm mid-grey, not near-black.**
v1 wondered whether the site's `#191008` theme colour indicated dark photography. Seven of
the nine images sit in a warm grey to greige family. Only two are dark. **[Observed]**

## What the reference actually does

### Three shot modes, used consistently

**Mode A — overhead on textured stone.**
Dead overhead, 90°. The object sits on a mottled grey-beige stone or concrete slab with its
texture plainly visible. No sweep, no gradient. Very soft light, a gentle shadow falling
lower-left. Used for plates, and for nested sets photographed stacked inside one another
rather than lined up. **[Observed]**

**Mode B — low angle on a seamless sweep.**
The signature shot. Camera roughly **15 to 25° above** the object, close to eye level, so
almost none of the interior is visible. Warm grey seamless sweep. Single soft key from
camera-left and slightly behind, shadow falling to the right. Gradient across the ground as
described above. **[Observed]**

**Mode C — styled scene, shallow depth of field.**
Objects among open books, a beaded trivet, a spoon. Roughly 45°, close, foreground falling
out of focus. Window light from upper left. This is the same family as the studio-table shots
from the video screenshots. **[Observed]**

### Consistent across all modes

- **Light is always upper-left.** Shadow falls right or lower-right in every image with a
  readable shadow. **[Observed]**
- **A grounding shadow is always present.** Nothing floats. **[Observed]**
- **The object is small in frame.** Roughly a third to a half of the frame width, base sitting
  around two-thirds to three-quarters down, so there is more empty space above than below.
  **[Observed, by eye]**
- **Object and ground sit close together in tone.** Cream on warm grey, tan on brown,
  speckled cream on greige. Low subject-to-ground contrast, the whole frame in one narrow
  warm-neutral family. The black mug on grey is the single exception. **[Observed]**

**This last point is doing more work than the gradient, and it costs nothing.** The
collection reads as one body because every frame lives in the same narrow palette. It is a
decision about backdrop colour, not a technique.

### The two dark images

One is a tan mug on deep brown with a harder light and a defined cast shadow. One is a bowl
on greige against a pure black background with hard, almost sunlit shadows. Both are handsome
and both are a different mode. **Opinion, labelled as such:** if coherence across a
collection page is the goal, pick one ground family and stay in it. The dark shots read as a
separate series rather than as part of the same run.

### What the reference does not solve

Every object in it is opaque, matte or semi-matte ceramic, and where a glaze is glossy the
specular highlights are simply left blown out as white streaks. That is fine on a pot. It is
not fine across the surface of a painting. **The reference offers no guidance on glare, and
cannot, because it never had the problem.** **[Observed]**

## The two shots your work needs

Unchanged from v1, and confirmed rather than contradicted by the reference.

### The record shot

A document, not a photograph. Flat-on, evenly lit, colour accurate, no gradient. What a buyer
judges the piece by, what the monthly studies release needs in an identical crop, what the
annual book is assembled from.

- Work parallel to the sensor. Use the camera's electronic level, not the eye.
- Camera centred on the middle of the work, at the height of the middle of the work.
- Two daylight LED panels at 45°, equal distance and height either side.
- All other light off. Curtains closed, or shoot after dark.
- Grey card in the first frame of every session. Correct to it in Lightroom.
- Shoot RAW. Same crop every month.

**On glare:** the reliable fix is cross-polarisation, a polarising filter on the lens plus
polarising sheets over both panels, rotated until reflections disappear. Costs about a stop
and adds setup time. Unnecessary for matte work on paper. Test before buying. **[Reasoned]**

### The presentation shot

Mode A or Mode B, depending on the object.

- **One** soft key light, camera-left, slightly above and slightly behind the object.
- Seamless sweep for Mode B, curving from vertical to horizontal with no visible corner.
- Textured stone or concrete slab for Mode A.
- Camera 15 to 25° above the object for Mode B, 90° for Mode A.
- Object small in frame, base around two-thirds down, headroom above.
- Ground tone chosen to sit close to the work's dominant tone.
- Portrait crop, roughly 9:10.

## Which product type gets which shot

| Product | Record shot | Presentation shot | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Painting | Required | Mode B, propped | Needs the panel edge visible to read as an object. Untested. **[Reasoned]** |
| Drawing | Required | Mode A, flat on the slab | Overhead on stone is the natural fit for unframed paper. **[Reasoned]** |
| Study | Required | Rarely | The monthly release runs on the record shot, identical crop, nothing else |
| Print | Required | Mode A or B | Reads well flat on the slab, or leaning in a loose stack **[Reasoned]** |
| Book | Optional | Mode B | Already has volume. Takes the reference look directly **[Reasoned]** |

**Mode A is probably your signature shot.** Small works on paper lying on a stone slab, shot
from directly overhead in soft light, is the closest thing in the reference to your actual
material, and it needs no sweep and no answer to the propping problem.

## Building it in the front room

The room has bookshelves on the far wall, two desks, and one window.

**A clean division that uses what's there:**

- **Window light for the presentation shot.** Modes A, B and C in the reference all look like
  soft directional daylight, and the presentation shot does not need to be repeatable month
  to month. Position the surface so the window falls camera-left. **[Reasoned]**
- **LED panels for the record shot.** This one does need to be repeatable, and window light
  in Surrey in November is not window light in July. Curtains closed, panels only.

Both live in the same corner on the same desk. Neither requires assembling anything beyond
laying down a slab or a sweep.

**Materials, cheap end:**

- Mode A ground: a paving slab, a concrete offcut, or a large matte porcelain tile in warm grey.
- Mode B sweep: a roll of matte paper or a painted board in warm mid-grey, curved against a wall.
- Diffusion, if the window light is ever hard: a white sheet or tracing paper taped up.

## Still open

- **[Open]** Exact ground colour. "Warm mid-grey" is as close as the eye gets from a
  screenshot. Worth buying two or three paper colours and testing against actual work.
- **[Open]** Whether flat work propped against a sweep (Mode B) reads well or looks awkward.
  Nothing in the reference tests this. Try it before committing.
- **[Open]** Whether work on paper is photographed or scanned for the record shot. Carried
  over from `documentation.html`. Scanning is more accurate and slower.
- **[Open]** Which desk becomes the permanent documentation position.
- **[Open]** Whether to use the dark ground at all, or hold to one family.

## Fit with the year as agreed

The Monday block goes to documentation rather than video. Nothing here changes that.

1. **Build the record shot first.** The studies release, the catalogue PDF and the annual
   book all depend on it. The panels are already on the gear list.
2. **Add Mode A second.** It is the cheapest presentation shot, needs only a slab and the
   window, and suits work on paper directly.
3. **Mode B last,** once the propping question has been answered.

**The governing constraint:** whatever is built has to live set up. A rig that needs
assembling will not get used. Same finding as the camera position, same reason.

**One thing worth watching.** Mode C, the styled scene, is the one that invites props, and
props are how a documentation session becomes a photoshoot. If a shot starts taking a Sunday,
it has stopped being documentation.
